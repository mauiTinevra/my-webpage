How six websites were built at the same time.

The six sites in this gallery were built in parallel by AI agents (Claude, from Anthropic), each working alone from a written brief, with one director setting the standard and reviewing the result. The six builds ran side by side and each took between 18 and 22 minutes. This page is the whole method, so anyone can repeat it.

One director, six builders

The director never wrote a line of any site. Its job was to write one document, BRIEFS.md, and then to look hard at what came back. The document has two parts: a build standard every site must meet, and six briefs.

Each brief names the business, the customer's errand, the look, an exact palette, two typefaces, one signature moment and the thing the site has to prove. Each builder got one brief and one folder, and was told not to touch anything else. Nothing is shared between sites: no template, no components, no common stylesheet. That is why a record shop printed like a poster sits next to a bookshop set like a quarterly and they don't look related.

The build standard

Twelve rules, the same for every site. The ones that mattered most:

  • The customer's errand comes first. The first screen says what the business is, where it is, and gives the one action a customer came for.
  • One signature moment, built properly. It has to really work, with a keyboard too, and not be a looping decoration.
  • Drawings are code. No stock photos and no generated images. Every illustration is SVG, canvas or CSS.
  • Real copy only. No placeholder text. Prices, hours and addresses read like a real shop's.
  • Honest about being imaginary. A bar at the top of every site says so, and phone numbers and emails can't reach anyone.
  • Plain files. HTML, CSS and JavaScript, fonts hosted with the site, zero console errors, no sideways scrolling on a phone.

The critique loop

A builder cannot judge a page it has not looked at. So every builder had to run the same small tool three times, open the screenshots, criticise them like a design director, fix what it found, and then add one deliberate improvement.

 "fontsLoaded":["Playfair Display","Source Serif 4"],"consoleErrors":[]}

The tool renders the page at desktop and phone sizes in headless Chrome, saves four screenshots, and reports console errors, page height, sideways overflow and which fonts actually loaded. Things the loop caught that nobody would have guessed from the code:

  • On the barber's sign, "Camelback" ran off the edge of the enamel.
  • On the bookshop shelf, spine titles had shrunk to about 8 pixels and most authors' names were dropped.
  • At the florist, stems poked out through the sides of the vase at twenty flowers.
  • At the record shop, the play button sat below the sleeves, so pressing it scrolled the turntable out of view.
  • On the star chart, labels overlapped, and the tour rule called a night moonless when a full moon rose mid-tour.
  • All six had the same hidden fault: a font preload that threw an error when the page was opened from disk.

The director's review

When the builders reported back, the director rendered every site again with the same tool and looked at each one at both sizes before anything was published. All six came back with no console errors, no sideways scrolling and their own fonts loaded. One fix was made at this stage: the notice bar on the stargazing site wrapped awkwardly on a phone.

Every site documents itself

Each site has its own guide, written in its own visual language: the palette, the typefaces, how the signature moment works with a piece of the real code, what each critique pass found, and one honest limitation. Start with the star chart's, the turntable's or the potter's wheel's.

Repeat it

  1. Write a build standard that is not negotiable, and keep it short enough to be read.
  2. Write real briefs: a business, an errand, a palette, typefaces, one signature moment, one thing to prove.
  3. Give each builder one brief and one folder. Share nothing between them.
  4. Make looking at screenshots mandatory, three times, with fixes each time.
  5. Review every result yourself, at phone and desktop size, before publishing.
  6. Ship plain static files.

What is and isn't real

The businesses, people, records and books are invented, and no order or booking is sent anywhere. The astronomy is real and computed on the page, with its accuracy stated in that site's guide. The record shop's music was checked by measuring levels, not by ear, because the build machine has no speakers. The sites were built by AI agents from briefs; a site made for a real client starts with a conversation and is designed and built with that client.
